Fuelling your first 10k

What to eat the week before, the morning of, and — the part nobody talks about — in the hour after you finish.

Ten kilometres is a wonderful distance. It's long enough that what you ate matters, and short enough that you don't need a nutrition plan with a spreadsheet attached. Most of the women I work with are doing their first one, and most of them are nervous about exactly the wrong things.

So here's the whole thing, in order.

The week before

Nothing dramatic. This is the week to stop experimenting, not start. Every new supplement, every "race week detox", every food you've never eaten before is a risk with no upside.

  • Keep eating the way you normally do, with a little more carbohydrate in the two or three days before — rice, bread, potato, pasta, fruit. You're topping up the fuel in your muscles, not stuffing yourself.
  • Drink to thirst across the week and check the colour of your urine: pale straw is the target, not clear.
  • Ease off very high-fibre foods in the last day or two if your stomach is sensitive. Lentils the night before a race is a decision you make once.

The night before

A normal dinner you've eaten a hundred times, slightly higher in carbohydrate, moderate in fat, not enormous. Rice with chicken. Pasta with a simple sauce. Something you know.

You are not going to fix months of training with one meal, and you can absolutely ruin a morning with one.

The morning of

The general guidance for endurance events is a carbohydrate-focused meal one to four hours before the start, sized to how much time you have. The more time before the gun, the bigger it can be.

  • 3–4 hours before: a proper breakfast — bread or oats, a little protein, some fruit.
  • 1–2 hours before: something small and simple — a banana, toast with honey or jam, a small bowl of cereal.
  • Under an hour: a banana or a date or two, if anything at all.

Keep fat and fibre low in that last meal — they slow stomach emptying, and a slow stomach at kilometre four is its own kind of misery. Drink around 400–600 ml of water in the couple of hours beforehand, then stop drinking large volumes right before the start.

If you normally drink coffee, drink your coffee. Race morning is not the day to find out what you're like without it.

The golden rule of race-day nutrition: nothing new. Not the food, not the drink, not the shoes, not the sports bra.

During the race

Here's the part that surprises people: for a 10k, most runners don't need to eat anything at all. Carbohydrate feeding during exercise starts to matter for efforts lasting beyond roughly an hour — below that, your existing glycogen stores have it covered, assuming you ate properly beforehand.

Water at the stations if it's warm or if you're out there longer than an hour. That's genuinely it. Gels are not a rite of passage.

After you finish

This is the part nobody talks about, and it's the part that decides how you feel tomorrow.

Within a couple of hours of finishing, aim for a meal containing carbohydrate to refill what you used, and around 20–30 g of protein to support recovery. Eggs and bread. Labneh with fruit and nuts. Chicken and rice. A glass of milk is a perfectly respectable recovery drink and considerably cheaper than the powder version.

Then keep drinking across the afternoon, not all at once. And eat a normal dinner. The most common recovery mistake I see isn't the wrong protein — it's women who "earned" the race and then barely eat for the rest of the day, and wonder why they feel wrecked on Monday.

Three things to skip

  • Cutting carbohydrate in race week. Low-carb training may have a place in some athletes' programmes; the week of your first 10k is not it.
  • A new supplement you saw online. If it worked as advertised, you'd have heard about it from your doctor and not from an ad.
  • Racing fasted because a video told you to. If you want to train some sessions in a fasted state, that's a conversation for your training block, not for race morning.

The honest summary

Eat normally. Add a bit more carbohydrate in the two or three days before. Have something small and familiar on the morning. Drink water. Don't try anything new. Eat a real meal afterwards.

Everything else is detail — and detail is what the individual plan is for, because your stomach, your schedule, your medication and your training load are not the same as anyone else's.

This article is general information, not individual advice. If you have a health condition, take medication, are pregnant, or have a history of disordered eating, please work with a dietitian directly.
